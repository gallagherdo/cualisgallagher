Topic: Project Gutenberg

Context

Project Gutenberg is an online database hosting nearly 80,000 freely available ebooks, focusing primarily on works no longer under copyright and/or of significant historical/cultural value. The project prides itself on offering its materials easily and without cost to users. It was started in 1971 by Michael S. Hart, who created the first ebook by transcribing a copy of the Declaration of Independence and uploading the transcription onto ARPANET, the precursor to the modern internet. Today Project Gutenberg is hosted by ibiblio at UNC Chapel Hill, offering books through readable online web pages or free downloadable versions.

Users

While Project Gutenberg prides itself on the massive accessibility of its ebooks, it acknowledges that some content featured in its library isn’t suitable for minors or may trigger psychological/physiological responses in certain readers. The website states on its Terms of Use page that its target audience is people in the United States over 13 years old. Project Gutenberg also states that it follows US copyright law and that international users (those outside the United States) should consult their own country’s copyright laws before accessing materials from Project Gutenberg’s library.

Content

Project Gutenberg sorts its massive catalog of ebooks into subject categories, starting with large-scale categories (Literature, Science & Technology, History, etc.) and deviating further into subcategories (under Literature: Adventure, Biographies, Novels, Poetry, etc.). There are also curated reading lists sorted by subject, language, or publication (such as a journal or newspaper). Users can select a subcategory to view ebooks sorted into this category, and may view books on the category’s homepage by popularity, release date (for Project Gutenberg; not the original publishing date), or author. Users may also search for specific ebooks using Project Gutenberg’s quick search bar in the banner at the top of the website or by using one of the advanced search options under the “Search Options” page.

Individual ebook records contain:

  • The work’s title
  • Author
  • Cover image
  • A “Read online now” button that takes users to the ebook’s HTML page
  • Various sharing and download links
  • Bibliographic information
    • Subject headings
    • ISBN
    • Copyright
    • Language
    • Credits (for the ebook version)
    • Reading level

Technical Features

Project Gutenberg’s focus on e-reading is clear not only in its collection of ebooks but also the various means of downloading ebooks, which come not only in plain text or HTML but also EPUB and MOBI formats. This makes ebooks highly accessible to e-readers like Kindles, Nooks, and Kobos, and Project Gutenberg also features step-by-step tutorials to help users transfer files downloaded from the Project onto their e-reader of choice. In 2020 Project Gutenberg underwent a significant graphical overhaul to modernize the website, remove depreciated and vulnerable content, and incorporate widespread HTML5+CSS to improve user experience. Another significant change was the increased use of number of downloads in search results or in reading lists or subject subcategories. By default, all pages will now display results sorted by number of downloads in the last 30 days, though sorting by title or author remains possible.

Pros/Cons

Project Gutenberg absolutely delivers on its promise of accessibility in its collection. Besides offering a variety of search options for more specialized users, sorting by popularity (number of downloads) makes it easy for users to snag the titles they are most likely looking for, since users searching for “Romeo and Juliet” will see Shakespeare’s play pushed to the top of their search results. Providing an HTML for reading online works for users on a desktop or who just need to quickly reference a work, while download links for plaintext, e-readers, and HTML offer other options for users depending on their preferences. It should be noted, though, that these downloads specialize in e-readers and reading apps, meaning that they can be consumed on other devices they may not function to their greatest effect. The readability of online sites has previously been a point of contention for Project Gutenberg. Without a reading app there are no page delineations, and combined with the limited formatting present on these pages text can become lumped together in large sections, stretched apart unnecessarily, and lack consistency across various editions of the same work. Some of this comes down to how the book was uploaded (either as a transcription or as a scan) but I also admit I have no experience with e-readers, so perhaps using one fixes a lot of the formatting as promised.Addressing text compatibility across devices was also a major goal of the 2020 website redesign, so the Project has taken strides to address usability.

Suggestions

Several important suggestions have been acknowledged by Project Gutenberg on their website redesign page, though the page is no longer maintained and at least some of the suggested changes have been applied. These include updates to Project Gutenberg’s “About” pages with more information about significant contributors, how donations are used, goals for the present and future, and a purpose statement. The website’s main About page as of writing does contain a mission statement, though it is unclear if this is purpose statement the Project noted it needed on the website redesign page. An example of an applied change are the suggested revisions to the “New Releases” tooltip on the website’s homepage that add the author to the book’s presented title and truncate the title to reduce the tooltip’s space on the page proper. Hovering over any new release will expand that book and reveal its full title and author. I offer two suggestions to search results pages that I would like to see: first, I would like to increase the number of search results displayed per page. Project Gutenberg currently caps the search results at 25 results per page, and I would like the option to increase this to 50 or perhaps 100. Second, I would like to see displayed the number of search results returned in a category or reading list. There doesn’t seem to be a way to view the total number of ebooks in a subject or reading list, so being able to browse a list can feel very open-ended or lacking direction without knowing how many options I have to browse.
